How do Kazakhstani sports fans decide whether foreign-born athletes who compete for Kazakhstan are truly “ours”?

InterpretivistNazarbayev UniversityFall 2026

The puzzle

One flag, many birthplaces

  • Kazakhstan has long recruited athletes born elsewhere, many from Russia. Rybakina switched in 2018, won Wimbledon in 2022, and in 2026 won the Australian Open and US Open and became world No. 1. (Rickleton & Medelbek, 2023; The National News Desk, 2026)
  • After each win by a foreign-born star, some fans ask why the country doesn’t develop its own talent. (Rickleton & Medelbek, 2023)
  • In 2024 the president called for ending the naturalization of foreign athletes. In 2025 Parliament banned budget funding for non-citizen athletes. (Askarov, 2025)

So when fans cheer for these athletes, or turn on them, what makes someone “ours”?

A second contrast

Born here, or brought here?

  • Born in Kazakhstan, not ethnically Kazakh

    e.g. Gennady Golovkin

    Do fans talk about ethnicity?

  • Born abroad, naturalized

    e.g. Rybakina, Bublik

    Do fans talk about birthplace and passports?

How, if at all, do fans talk differently about these two kinds of athletes when deciding who is “ours”?

A contrast to listen for, not a variable to measure. It links to Kazakhness and Kazakhstanness (Laruelle, 2014).

Where I stand

Interpretivism

Not

But

What does “ours” mean to fans?

Fans create the meaning of “ours”, so their own words are my evidence. (Schwartz-Shea & Yanow, 2012)

What others found, 1 of 2

Quiet in the media, loud among fans

  1. 1

    Switching has grown

    Most Olympic nationality switches came after the 1990s. Few were pure talent-for-passport deals. (Jansen et al., 2018)

  2. 2

    The press stays calm

    Most newspaper coverage treats switches casually. Controversy flares only in some cases. (Jansen, 2019)

  3. 3

    Fans do argue

    In China, fans’ support for naturalized players rises and falls with the team’s results. (Han, Tang & Bairner, 2024)

What others found, 2 of 2

...and an open question

  1. 4

    Belonging has two sides

    Feeling at home, and the politics of who is allowed to belong. (Antonsich, 2010; Yuval-Davis, 2006)

  2. 5

    Kazakhstan tells several stories

    State identity mixes ethnic Kazakhness, civic Kazakhstanness and a global brand. (Laruelle, 2014)

  3. 6

    A 2023 survey of 4,000 Kazakhstanis, plus interviews, on civic and ethnic belonging. (Sadvokassova, Burkhanov & Sharipova, 2025)

    The gap

    Fan studies exist for China, but not for Kazakhstan. What conditions do Kazakhstani fans attach to “ours”?

Analytical framework: expectations

Expectations

  1. 1

    “Ours” may depend on results: praised after a win, called “Russian” after a loss.

  2. 2

    Speaking Kazakh may matter more than birthplace to some fans.

  3. 3

    Athletes who switched after 2022 may be judged more harshly than earlier ones.

  4. 4

    Some fans may not care: athletes as professionals, not national symbols.

  5. 5

    Fans may accept Kazakhstan-born athletes more easily than foreign-born ones, whatever their ethnicity.

Analytical framework: positionality

How I relate to this topic

What helps me

  • I’m a Kazakhstani citizen, and this question matters to me.
  • I’m a tennis fan, so I know the players, the matches and where fans argue.
  • I can read comments in both Kazakh and Russian.

What I need to watch for

  • Because I care, I may hear the answer I expect.
  • As a fan, I may share other fans’ assumptions without noticing.
  • Loud commenters may not speak for most fans.
  • Interviewees may give the “patriotic” answer.

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don’t understand about my research design

What working with AI helped me understand

  • A passport makes an athlete eligible. It doesn’t make them “ours”.
  • Belonging may be conditional, not yes or no.

What I still don’t understand
